What Happens When Your Eyes Aren’t Protected

Most people know UV rays are bad for their skin, and that’s why they take their sunscreen with them. A few others consider what those same rays do to their eyes when nothing blocks them.

The most immediate risk of exposing your eyes to UV rays is photokeratitis. This term refers to a sunburn on the surface of your eye. It shows up as redness, tearing, and a gritty, painful sensitivity to light, usually within hours of heavy sun exposure. A bad day at Rockaway without proper eye protection is enough to trigger it.

The long-term problems are usually quieter. Years of unprotected UV exposure speed up the development of cataracts, as the eye’s lens gradually clouds over and vision starts to decline. It also increases the risk of damage to the part of your retina that handles your central and sharpest vision, called the macula.

The tricky part is that neither of those gives you much warning, and by the time you notice something is off, the damage has been building for a while.

UV400 Is the Number to Look For

When you’re shopping for sunglasses, you’ll see labels like “UV protection” or “blocks UV rays” on a lot of pairs, including cheap ones. Those phrases don’t mean anything much except the ones with “UV400.”

UV400 means the lenses block all ultraviolet light up to 400 nanometers, which covers the full range of both UVA (Ultraviolet A) and UVB (Ultraviolet B) rays. A label that just says “UV protection” could mean anything from partial coverage to a coating that wears off after a few months. UV400 is the specific standard worth looking for.

The other thing worth knowing is that a dark tint has nothing to do with UV protection. A very dark lens without a UV rating can actually make things worse because your pupils dilate more in the shade, letting harmful rays through. The darkness fools your eye into relaxing its natural defenses.

Polarized Lenses and UV Protection Are Not the Same Thing

Polarized lenses cut glare. When sunlight bounces off the water at Rockaway or off the wet sand at Coney Island, polarized lenses filter that harsh reflected light so your vision stays comfortable and clear. For a beach day in Queens, that’s genuinely useful.

However, polarization does not block UV rays. A polarized lens with no UV rating does not protect your eye health; it only protects your comfort. Those are two different things, and confusing them is one of the most common mistakes people make when buying sunglasses.

The lens worth having has both UV400 protection and polarization effect. That combination handles the full picture, especially near water, where UV reflects upward and hits your eyes from angles a standard frame won’t always cover.

If You Wear Contacts, Read This Before the Beach

Many contact lens wearers assume their lenses handle UV exposure, especially if the box says “UV protection.” Unfortunately, that label covers only your cornea. The white of your eye, your eyelids, and all the skin around your eyes are still fully exposed. UV-blocking contacts are a useful layer, but they were never designed to replace sunglasses.

There’s also the ocean water issue, and this one matters more. Ocean water, pool water, and even water from a garden hose carry microorganisms that can get trapped between your lens and your eye. In the ocean, specifically, that contact lens becomes a direct delivery mechanism for bacteria straight to your cornea.

The practical answer is simple:

 

  • Wear UV400 sunglasses over your contacts, always
  • If you’re going in the water, take the contacts out first
  • Pack a lens case and solution, not just your glasses as backup

If you wear contacts and spend time near water in Queens or anywhere in the city during the summer, this is worth talking through at your next visit. The right setup depends on your prescription and how your eyes handle heat and dry air.

New York City Reflects More UV Than You Think

Living in New York puts your eyes at a disadvantage in summer, and it has nothing to do with the weather. When you walk Austin Street on a bright day, you’re picking up UV bouncing off the pavement, off the glass storefronts, and off the cars beside you. By the time you get to Rockaway, Jones Beach, or Coney Island, add the water and the sand to that list. Water reflects UV by up to 50%, which can effectively double your total exposure compared to just standing in an open field.

A wide-brimmed hat cuts direct sun reaching your eyes by up to 50%, and it’s worth bringing. It won’t do anything about the UV reflecting off the water and hitting you from the front, which is why it works alongside proper sunglasses, not instead of them.

A Few Things Worth Checking Before Shopping

The UV400 label is the starting point. If the pair you’re looking at doesn’t show it, put it down. Also, price doesn’t tell you anything here. A $20 pair with UV400 does more for your eyes than a $200 pair without it.

Frame size is another extremely important factor. A small lens leaves a lot of room for UV light to enter from the sides and above, which makes larger frames and wraparound styles better alternatives. If you’re spending time near the water in Queens, that coverage makes a real difference because the light hits you from multiple directions at once.

Polarization is worth adding, especially for a beach day at Rockaway or Jones Beach. It cuts the reflected glare off the water so your eyes aren’t working overtime. Just confirm the lens is UV400-rated as well, because polarization alone doesn’t give you UV protection.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need sunglasses if it’s cloudy?

Yes. UV rays pass through cloud cover. A grey sky in July still carries enough UV to cause damage, especially near water, where it reflects off the surface regardless of how bright the sun looks.

Are expensive sunglasses better for UV protection?

Price has nothing to do with it. A $20 pair with a UV400 label protects your eyes. A $300 pair without it doesn’t. The label is the only thing that tells you what the lens is actually doing.

Can I wear my contacts to the beach?

You can wear them to the beach, but take them out before you go in the water. Ocean water carries bacteria that can get trapped between the lens and your eye. Always bring a case and solution, and wear UV400 sunglasses over your contacts the whole time you’re outside.

Do my UV-blocking contacts replace sunglasses?

No. UV-blocking contacts cover only your cornea. The white of your eye, your eyelids, and the skin around your eyes are still fully exposed. Sunglasses are still necessary.

Is polarization the same as UV protection?

No. Polarization cuts glare from reflective surfaces like water, which makes it useful at the beach. But it does not block UV rays. A polarized lens still needs a UV400 rating to protect your eye health.

Can I get prescription lenses in sunglasses?
