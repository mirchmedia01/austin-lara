Your eyes probably feel like sandpaper after hours of working. There’s a dull pressure behind your temples that wasn’t there at lunch, and every time you look away from your screen, it takes longer than usual to refocus. Your glasses are fine. You’ve just been staring at screens since 9 am.

Those are symptoms of Computer Vision Syndrome, or CVS. It covers dry eyes, blurred vision, tension headaches, and neck pain that builds over the course of the day. Remote workers, students, and office staff across Queens and NYC are dealing with it more than ever, and most are managing it with habits that only go halfway.

Why 2026 Is Different

A few years ago, office workers were physically active and spent 40 minutes commuting to and from work. No screen, no notifications, and no reason to focus at close range was doing more for eye health than most people realized. When remote work began, workers removed it almost entirely, and screen hours filled the gap.

The average worker is now on a device for the better part of ten hours a day, switching between a work laptop, a phone, and a television in a small apartment with whatever lighting was already there. Remote or hybrid work is not bad, but the eyes were not built for sustained close focus. The strain that used to show up occasionally now appears every afternoon.

Symptoms of Computer Vision Syndrome: What CVS Actually Feels Like

Most people blame the headache on bad sleep, or the dry eyes on the weather, or the neck tension on their chair. It rarely occurs to anyone that all three started at the same time when they sat down for eight hours of back-to-back calls. By the time the connection becomes obvious, it’s been building for weeks. These are the signs that usually point to digital eye strain:

  • Dry, burning, or gritty eyes by mid-afternoon
  • Blurred vision or slow refocusing after looking away from a screen
  • Headaches behind the eyes or at the temples
  • Neck and shoulder tension that worsens throughout the day
  • Sensitivity to overhead lighting or screen glare

If three or more of these show up regularly, something specific is causing them. Finding out what it is matters more than managing the symptoms.

How to Relieve Digital Eye Strain: Five Things That Work

Almost everyone on the internet suggests that you need to “take more breaks” or “get more rest” to fix computer vision syndrome. That’s not wrong, but it’s incomplete. Screen strain has different drivers, and not all of them respond to the same fix. The five things below address the actual mechanics of why your eyes struggle with screens, not just the surface symptoms.

The 20-20-20 Rule

You’ve probably heard this one before: every 20 minutes, look at something 20 feet away for 20 seconds. As simple as that may seem, it is an extremely effective way to counter digital eye strain. However, what kills the effectiveness is treating it like a casual glance. Your focusing muscle needs a genuine reset, not a two-second look at the wall across your desk. The object needs to be genuinely far, a window view, a building across the street, anything that forces your eyes to shift out of near-focus completely.

Anti-Reflective Coatings

This fix gets the least attention online, and it makes a bigger difference than people expect. The glare from office lights hits uncoated lenses all day and quietly wears your eyes out. Zeiss and Crizal make coatings that cut that glare down, and by the end of the day, your eyes will feel it. If your glasses don’t have an AR coating, that’s worth dealing with before anything else.

Screen Position and Lighting

In many cases, your screen height and the light in your room do more damage than your screen time itself. The ideal setup places your monitor slightly below eye level and about an arm’s length away. This keeps your neck neutral and reduces the effort your eyes must exert to maintain focus. The problem for a lot of NYC workers is that home offices are whatever space was available, a kitchen counter, a couch, a desk crammed into a bedroom corner, none of which were designed with eye comfort in mind. Adjusting your screen position costs nothing, and it removes one of the biggest drivers of end-of-day strain.

Preservative-Free Eye Drops

Staring at a screen makes people blink much less than usual, and that dries their eyes out faster than most expect. Eye drops help, but not all of them are the same. Regular drops used too often can irritate the eye surface over time due to the preservatives they contain. The preservative-free ones are the better call, and keeping them on the desk makes it easier to use them before things get uncomfortable.

Deliberate Blinking

Eyes normally blink between 15 and 20 times a minute. As mentioned earlier, the blink rate drops to around 5 times per minute on a screen. The tear film that keeps eyes comfortable needs full blinks to stay intact, and the half-blinks that occur during screen time do little. Making a point to blink fully and slowly every few minutes costs nothing and makes a real difference by the end of the day.

Frequently Asked Questions on Digital Strain

What is computer vision syndrome?

Computer vision syndrome refers to eye problems caused by excessive screen time. Dry eyes, blurred vision, headaches, and neck pain that builds throughout the day are the most common signs.

Do blue light glasses help with digital eye strain?

Current research finds no meaningful difference in eye comfort between blue-light-filtering lenses and regular lenses. An outdated prescription is a more common cause of screen strain than blue light.

How is a computer vision exam different from a regular eye exam?

A regular eye exam checks your overall vision and eye health. A computer vision exam specifically assesses how your eyes function at screen distance. This includes focusing ability and eye coordination, which a standard checkup may not catch.

How long does digital eye strain take to go away?

For most people, symptoms ease within a few hours of stepping away from screens. If strain persists into the next morning or worsens each day, that is a sign that something beyond screen habits needs to be addressed.

Can eye strain cause headaches?

Yes. The muscle fatigue from holding focus at screen distance for hours creates tension that often shows up as a headache behind the eyes or at the temples. It is one of the most common complaints among remote workers and students.

Does screen time permanently damage your eyes?

Current evidence says no. Screen use does not cause permanent eye damage. What it does cause is real, cumulative discomfort that affects how well you function day-to-day, and that is worth addressing.
