Back-to-school season in Queens is one of the busiest times of year. School supplies, new sneakers, and pediatrician visits are usually at the top of the list for most parents. Eye exams are often the last to be done or left off entirely. But for a child starting kindergarten or first grade, or moving into a new school year with heavier reading demands, a missed eye exam could mean spending the whole year straining to see what they should see easily.

Although schools in the US perform vision screenings, they are not eye exams. And the difference between those two things can significantly affect how your child performs in the classroom.

School Vision Screenings Aren’t Eye Exams

The school nurse or counselor who checks your child’s vision at school is doing a quick pass for obvious distance problems. They’re simply looking for kids who can’t see the board from the back of the room.

They’re not checking how well your child’s eyes work together, how they track across a line of text, how they focus when shifting between a book and a whiteboard, their depth perception, the health of the eye itself, or early signs of conditions like lazy eye.

According to the American Optometric Association, school vision screenings can miss up to 75% of children with vision problems. Passing a school screening does not mean the child does not have a vision problem serious enough to affect their reading, attention span, and confidence in class.

What Poor Vision Looks Like in a Classroom

Children rarely say, “I can’t see.” They don’t have a reference point for what a clear vision should feel like. So what you see instead are behaviors associated with sight.

If your child showcases the following symptoms, a proper eye exam is inevitable:

  • Tilts their head or squints when reading
  • Loses their place frequently while reading aloud
  • Avoids reading or complains of headaches after schoolwork
  • Holds books very close to their face
  • Gets tired or frustrated with homework quickly
  • Has trouble copying from the board

When a child can’t see clearly, they can easily fall behind. They may appear to have attention issues or a lack of interest when the real problem is their eyesight. Children are frequently referred for ADHD evaluations when the underlying issue is a vision problem that hasn’t been caught.

As much as 80% of learning in the classroom relies on vision. Reading, writing, copying notes, and tracking a moving object all depend on a visual system that’s working correctly. One weak link in that chain is enough to slow a child down.

The Conditions That Only Show Up in a Real Exam

There are a few conditions, in particular, that school screenings will not catch and that have a narrow treatment window.

Amblyopia (lazy eye) is one of them. If amblyopia isn’t diagnosed and treated by around age 9 or 10, the vision loss in the affected eye can become permanent. The brain stops developing the connection with that eye, and by the time it becomes an obvious problem, the window for full correction may have closed.

Convergence insufficiency is another. This is when the eyes can’t work together smoothly when focusing on close objects. A child with this condition sees double or blurry when reading, which makes sustained reading exhausting. It’s incredibly common and almost never caught in a school screening.

Myopia (nearsightedness) is progressing faster in children today, partly driven by increased screen time and reduced outdoor activity. A child who needs glasses and doesn’t have them isn’t just squinting through class. Their prescription is likely getting worse with every month it goes uncorrected.

A comprehensive exam catches all of these.

When Should Your Child’s Eyes Be Checked?

According to the American Optometric Association, a child’s first eye examination should be completed by 6 months old. A follow-up exam is advised at age 3, then once again before beginning school, typically around age 5. If no vision issues are detected, eye exams should occur at least every two years. For children with prescriptions, a family history of eye concerns, or learning challenges, yearly exams are recommended.

FAQ On Back-to-School Eye Exam

Does my child need an eye exam if they passed the school vision screening? 

Yes. School screenings only check basic distance vision. A comprehensive exam looks at how the eyes work together, how they track across text, how they focus, and the overall health of the eye. A child can pass a school screening and still have a vision problem that’s slowing them down in class.

At what age should kids get their first eye exam? 

The American Optometric Association recommends the first exam by 6 months of age, then again at age 3, and once more before starting school at around age 5. After that, every one to two years, depending on what the exam finds.

What are the signs my child might have a vision problem? 

Squinting, tilting the head to look at something, losing their place while reading, avoiding books, sitting too close to the TV, frequent headaches after homework, or rubbing their eyes a lot. The tricky part is that children often don’t say anything because they don’t know what a clear vision is supposed to feel like.

Can a vision problem be mistaken for ADHD or a learning disability? 

Yes, and it happens more often than most parents realize. When a child can’t see clearly or their eyes aren’t working together properly, they get distracted, frustrated, and disengaged. That pattern looks a lot like attention issues. A proper eye exam is a reasonable first step before any referral for behavioral evaluation.

What’s the difference between an optometrist and an optician? 

An optometrist is a doctor who examines the eyes, diagnoses conditions, and writes prescriptions. An optician is a licensed specialist who fills those prescriptions and fits glasses or contact lenses. How long does a children’s eye exam take? 

Usually 30 to 45 minutes. It depends on the child’s age and whether any follow-up testing is needed. Younger children may take a little longer since the process is adapted to keep them comfortable.

My child is scared of eye doctors. What should I do? 

It helps to tell them what to expect beforehand: the doctor will show them pictures or letters, shine a small light near their eyes, and ask them questions. Nothing hurts.
