What Does “Hard to Fit” Mean?

A hard-to-fit eye is one in which a standard soft lens does not sit correctly, remain comfortable, or provide clear vision, usually because of the eye’s shape, its tear film, or a corneal condition. The most common causes are keratoconus, high or irregular astigmatism, chronic dry eye, and corneas reshaped by surgery.

Astigmatism happens when the cornea curves unevenly rather than forming a smooth sphere, which scatters light and blurs vision unless the lens is shaped to counteract it. Keratoconus happens when the cornea thins over time and bulges into a cone-like shape, and standard lenses simply cannot hold a stable position on that surface. Dry eye adds a separate problem. Since a lens needs a healthy tear layer underneath it to stay comfortable, eyes that do not produce enough tears or produce poor-quality ones will reject almost any lens that touches the cornea directly.

Why the Fitting Fails at Other Practices

Most contact lens appointments are built around a handful of standard lens brands and a fifteen-to twenty-minute visit. That works fine for average eyes. It falls apart for anyone whose cornea does not match the assumptions on which those lenses were designed.

We have seen this pattern several times, and it usually goes like this: a patient tries one or two soft toric lenses, the fit rotates, or the vision stays blurry, and the provider concludes that contacts “just aren’t for” that patient. What actually happened is narrower. A limited set of lens designs was tried, without the corneal mapping needed to understand why they failed or what a better-shaped lens would need to look like. Fitting a genuinely hard-to-fit eye usually requires equipment most general practices do not keep on hand, along with access to specialty lens labs that build lenses to exact measurements rather than pulling from a standard inventory.

Matching the Right Lens to the Right Eye

There is no single best lens for hard-to-fit eyes. The right choice depends on the specific condition, its severity, and the patient’s eyes’ response to the trial fitting.

Scleral lenses cover the entire cornea and rest on the white part of the eye. They are often the top choice for conditions such as keratoconus, post-surgery corneas, and moderate-to-severe dry eye because they keep the eye moist throughout the day. Rigid gas-permeable lenses remain a strong option for irregular astigmatism and can outlast soft lenses by a wide margin. However, they take longer to adjust to. Hybrid lenses have a firm center for clear vision and a soft edge for comfort, which gives you sharp vision like RGP lenses but with an easier adjustment. For a deeper look at how scleral lenses address keratoconus and severe dry eye, it is worth reviewing the specifics of how fitting is measured and adjusted.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I wear contact lenses if I have astigmatism?

In most cases, yes. Mild to moderate astigmatism is usually correctable with a soft toric lens. Higher or irregular astigmatism often needs a toric RGP or hybrid lens, which holds its shape more precisely than a soft lens can.

What is the best contact lens option for keratoconus?

Scleral lenses are the most common recommendation because they vault over the irregular cornea entirely and rest on the sclera, avoiding contact with the thinned or bulging area. RGP lenses are also used for milder cases, depending on the severity of the keratoconus.

Why do my contacts feel uncomfortable even though my prescription is correct?

Discomfort usually points to a fit or tear film issue rather than a prescription problem. A lens can be optically correct and still sit poorly on the eye or dry out faster than your tear film can keep up with, both of which a standard exam without topography can miss.

I was told I’m not a candidate for contacts. Is that actually true?

Not always, and often not at all. That conclusion is often based on a limited set of lens types being tried rather than a full evaluation of your specific corneal needs. A hard-to-fit consultation with corneal mapping can identify options a standard fitting would never reach.

Are scleral lenses right for me?

Scleral lenses tend to work well for keratoconus, post-surgical corneas, irregular astigmatism, and moderate to severe dry eye. Whether they are the right choice for your eyes specifically depends on your corneal shape and tear film, which the fitting evaluation assesses.

How long does a hard-to-fit contact lens fitting take?

The initial evaluation and trial lens fitting typically takes longer than a standard contact lens exam, often close to an hour. Most patients need at least one follow-up visit before the final lenses are ordered.
