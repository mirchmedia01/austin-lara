What to Watch for

A wrong prescription isn’t just annoying, it can affect your daily function. When vision is over- or under-corrected, the brain works harder to compensate. That strain can lead to:

  •  Persistent headaches
  • Dizziness or motion sensitivity
  •  Difficulty adapting to new glasses
  • Blurry vision that doesn’t improve with blinking
  •  Fatigue after short periods of reading or computer work

Ignoring these symptoms can prolong discomfort and interfere with driving, work, and productivity. Understanding the signs can help you get back to comfortable, clear vision faster.

The Symptom–Prescription Connection

A prescription can feel “wrong” for several reasons, and each has its own visual symptoms. Here’s a breakdown:

1. Headaches and Eye Strain

If your glasses feel “too strong,” “too weak,” or misaligned, the eyes must work overtime. This often shows up as tension around the temples or brow after using screens.

2. Dizziness or Motion Sickness

If your brain struggles to reconcile what the lenses are showing, you may feel off balance. This happens when the difference between your old and new prescription is too large or when astigmatism wasn’t measured accurately.

3. Blurry Vision With New Glasses

Some blur is normal during the adaptation period for new glasses, but continued blur may signal:

  •  Incorrect refraction
  •  Wrong optical center placement
  •  Poor frame alignment
  •  Uneven lens power (manufacturing error)

4. Trouble Focusing Between Distances

If you’re asking yourself, “Is my eyeglass prescription too strong?”, the problem may lie in the fine balance between objective and subjective refraction. A rushed exam or outdated prescription can throw off your near-to-distance transitions.

 

5. Dizziness When Switching to Progressive or Multifocal Lenses

Progressive lenses require precise measurements. If they’re off by even 1–2 mm, you may feel swim, distortion, or nausea.

Why Prescriptions Go Wrong

Austin Optics sees this more often than you might think. Common causes include:

  • Outdated prescriptions that don’t match your current refractive error
  •  Incorrect measurements during quick screenings or retail shop exams
  • Poor frame fit causing lenses to sit too high, low, or tilted
  •  Lens manufacturing errors
  • Self-diagnosed online orders with no professional verification

A prescription isn’t just a number—it’s a personalized visual system. That requires precision instruments and expertise.

What to Do Next

  • Pay attention to headaches, blur, or dizziness after getting new glasses
  • Don’t assume discomfort is “normal”—adaptation should not take weeks
  • Book a comprehensive eye exam to verify prescription accuracy
  • Ask about frame adjustments; incorrect positioning can distort vision
